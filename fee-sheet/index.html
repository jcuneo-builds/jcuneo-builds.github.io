Fee Sheet Builder

Replacing a formula-heavy pricing spreadsheet with an app that prices every team in code, checks it against resourcing rules, gets it approved and files the final version where everyone can find it. A Claude connection learns from past deals to suggest smarter teams and find the people with capacity to staff them.

The problem

One spreadsheet, a hundred ways to break it

Every new deal and every renegotiation needs a fee sheet: who works on the account, where they sit, how much of their time it takes, and what the client pays for it. Before this project, each one started as a copy of a shared Google Sheet template with thousands of formulas behind it.

Before

Copy the template Overwrite a formula by accident "v3 FINAL (2)" in someone's Drive Approval in a Slack thread Tracker updated by hand

A fee sheet was only as reliable as the last person who edited it. Nobody could say which copy was final, and the pricing rules lived in people's heads.

After

Every number computed in code Final versions locked Resourcing checks on every edit Approvals in one place Tracker fills itself

Pick roles, locations and time. The app works out the fee, margin and ratios, flags anything outside the rules, collects approvals and files the final version, with a full history behind it.

The spreadsheet worked, in the sense that it produced a number. But one pasted value over a formula could change a price without anyone seeing, and nobody would know until finance saw the margin months later. Rates, exchange rates and job titles were copied into every file, so an update to the template never reached the hundreds of copies already in circulation. Version control meant file names.

Review was just as scattered. The commercial team checked each sheet against a set of resourcing principles (a client lead on every account, enough senior time, no teams stacked with directors) by reading it line by line. Approvals happened in chat threads, and a separate deal tracker had to be updated by hand with the approved fee once everything was signed off.

The brief: make building a fee sheet easier and more accurate for everyone, keep a real version history, and keep every final fee sheet organized and findable. Keep salary and rate data away from anyone who doesn't need it, and never send anything to a client automatically.

The solution

The app owns the math

A web app on Google Cloud where people build a team row by row and the server does every calculation. Deals arrive from the CRM each morning. Several people can work on one fee sheet at once and see each other's changes live. Each deal keeps every version, and once a version is marked Final it can't be edited, only superseded. Before launch, the app and the old template were run on the same sheets until every number matched.

Inputs in, numbers out

People only ever choose things: a role, a location, a percentage of time, which costs to include. There are no formulas to type over, so there are none to break.

Rates never reach the browser

The ratecard and salary data live on the server. The browser gets the fee for each role and the totals, never the cost rates behind them, and only admins can see or change Settings.

Flag, don't block

Resourcing checks run on every edit and say exactly what is off and why. They inform the approver rather than stopping the sheet. The one hard stop is a role the ratecard can't price.

How it works

From deal to Final in nine steps

Every fee sheet follows the same path. The first six steps happen inside the app; the last three are where the fee sheet reaches other people.

The life of a fee sheet
1
Deal arrivesCRM sync, every morning

A scheduled job pulls clients and deals from the CRM, so a new pitch or renegotiation is already waiting on the homepage. Nobody retypes a client name, and each deal links back to its CRM record by ID.

2
Draft a first teamRules-based, optional

Enter media spend by channel and the app proposes a starting team: a client lead, an analytics role, and a pod per channel sized by that channel's spend. The same deal always gives the same draft, and nothing is added until a person chooses to add it.

3
Ask ClaudePast performance + capacity

Claude compares the deal with past fee sheets and how those accounts went, then suggests changes to the team: a role that similar accounts needed, a pod that was usually too thin. It also checks the resource planning and time tracking tools to name people at the right level with time free. Suggestions are just that; a person picks what to add.

4
Build the teamRole, location, FTE

Each row is a role from the job architecture, a location and a share of a person's time. Rows sort themselves by group, then practice, then seniority. Other editors on the same sheet see each change as it lands.

5
Set the commercialsModel, tech, third-party costs

Pick a commercial model (FTE, Hybrid or Commission), tick the technology and third-party costs to include, and optionally add a performance-based element on paid media fees. Scenarios let a team compare spend levels side by side.

6
ChecksResourcing rules, live

About twenty resourcing rules run on every edit: minimum role size, senior mix, a client lead on every account, analytics coverage, effective commission rate against a spend-based benchmark, margin floor. Admins can change thresholds or add rules in Settings without a deploy.

Everything above stays inside the app
7
Request approvalPosted to a review channel

A structured approval request goes to the commercial review channel in Slack with the fee, margin, ratios and any open checks. A ‚úÖ reaction from an approver records the approval in the app. Ordinary edits keep approvals; a change to the price clears them.

8
Mark FinalLocked on the server

Once the required approval is in, anyone on the deal can mark the version Final. The server refuses any further edit to it. A change means a new version, and the new Final supersedes the old one.

9
Filed and exportedTracker, shared drive, deck

The deal's row in the Notion tracker fills in with the fee sheet link, approved fee and key ratios. A copy of the Final lands in a view-only shared drive, replaced if it is ever superseded. Anyone can export the full fee sheet or a ready-made commercial deck.

Architecture

Five paths, one source of truth

The system splits into five independent paths. Labels A to E are separate flows, not one sequence. Firestore is the single store for deals, versions, settings and the audit log.

ABuild

Every edit, live

The browser sends small, typed edits ("set this row's FTE to 50%") rather than whole documents. The API checks each one against the sheet's rules, recalculates, saves to Firestore and pushes the result to everyone else on the sheet.

BSync

Every morning

Cloud Scheduler starts a Cloud Run Job with an OIDC identity. It reads clients, deals and owners from the CRM with a read-only token and updates Firestore. The job has no HTTP surface, and a person can also start a sync from the app.

CAdvise

On request, per fee sheet

The API gathers similar past fee sheets and how those accounts performed, and asks Claude what the new team is missing. It reads planned capacity and tracked time from the resourcing tools so suggestions come with real people who have room to take the work.

DPublish

On approval and Final

The API posts approval requests to the review channel and reads ‚úÖ reactions back as approvals. On Final it locks the version, fills the deal's tracker row in Notion and copies the Final into a view-only shared drive.

EDeploy

On merge to main

GitHub Actions authenticates to Google Cloud through Workload Identity Federation, with no stored keys. Terraform provisions every resource from locked, versioned state. A weekly routine checks the job architecture and opens a pull request when the role list changes.

Technical detail

Under the hood

Claude as a team advisor

Claude reads the deal alongside past fee sheets for similar accounts (spend, channels, term) and how those accounts went afterwards. It suggests where a team tends to be thin or overbuilt, and explains each suggestion with the past deals behind it. Every suggestion goes through the same checks as a hand-made edit.

Edits are operations, not documents

The browser never saves a whole fee sheet. It sends one small operation at a time, and the server decides whether it is allowed: a Final version refuses all of them, and only admins can approve or change Settings. Because the rules live on the server, a modified browser can't get around them.

Settings are versioned data

The ratecard, exchange rates, job titles, tech costs and check thresholds are all data that admins edit in the app. Each save is a numbered version with a preview of what it would change on open fee sheets, and a generic upload accepts a new rate table in one go.

Show the math

Any role can be expanded to show every step from ratecard to fee, with the formula written out beside each number. Reviewers stopped asking "where did this come from?" because the answer is one click away.

Pricing: a documented, deterministic formula

# calc.ts ¬∑ one team row
bill rate = salary/hr √ó (1 + overhead) √∑ (1 ‚àí margin)
hours = FTE √ó hours a year √ó term √∑ 12
fee = bill rate √ó hours √ó FX
staff cost = raw cost/hr √ó hours √ó FX
# the whole sheet
CGM = 1 ‚àí staff cost √∑ team fee
SCR = team fee √∑ staff cost
ECR = team fee √∑ media spend over the term

Margin divides, it doesn't multiply. Dividing by (1 ‚àí margin) means a 40% margin is 40% of the price, not a 40% markup on cost, which is what finance measures.

Spend is always annual. Media spend is entered per year even on a short term, and the app converts it to the term, so ECR compares like with like.

ECR has a benchmark. The effective commission rate is checked against a stepped benchmark by annual spend band, because what counts as reasonable falls as spend grows.

Privacy and security controls

  • Cost data stays on the serverThe ratecard and salary data are read only by the API. Non-admins never receive them, and real rates are never committed to the repository.
  • Signed in, every requestIdentity-Aware Proxy sits in front of the app, so only signed-in company accounts reach it, and an admin group controls Settings.
  • Final means finalA locked version refuses every edit on the server, not just in the interface. Changing a Final always creates a new version.
  • Everything auditedEvery edit, approval and settings change is written to an audit log with who made it and when.
  • Nothing goes to a clientThe app files and exports internally. A person still decides what is sent, through the existing approval process.
  • Formal sign-offThreat-modelled and approved with the security team before launch, with secrets in Secret Manager and infrastructure following the platform team's house pattern.

Rules first, then Claude

"Draft a first team" uses the same resourcing principles as the checks, so a draft has nothing for the checks to flag as an error. Each channel gets a pod sized by its own spend; shared roles are sized by the total. With a target fee or ECR, the pods scale to meet it, but never below the minimum role size. If the smallest team the rules allow still costs more than the target, the draft says so and keeps to the rules. Claude then works on top of that floor, so its suggestions start from a team that already passes the checks.

Engineering practice

Pure logic separated from I/O. Storage has an in-memory version and a Firestore version behind the same interface, so the whole app runs on a laptop with made-up rates and no credentials.

Automated tests cover the calculations, the checks, the first-draft rules, exports, approvals, the CRM sync and the Firestore security rules (against the emulator).

Infrastructure as code. Every resource is in Terraform and deployed through GitHub Actions.

Key impact

What changed

Accurate

Every fee sheet priced the same way, from the same rates

A ratecard or exchange rate change is made once in Settings and applies everywhere, with a preview of its effect before saving.

Findable

One place for every Final

The tracker row fills itself and a view-only copy lands in the shared drive, so "which version did we send?" has one answer.

Faster

A first team in one click

The rules-based draft gives a compliant starting point instead of a blank sheet.

Visible

Review built in

Checks, approvals and the reasoning behind each number sit on the sheet, so review time goes on judgement rather than arithmetic.

Smarter

Teams built on what worked

Claude turns past deals into suggestions and finds people with time free, so a team is shaped by evidence and staffable from day one.
